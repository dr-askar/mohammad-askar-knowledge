# Foveal hypoplasia: development, OCT grading and clinical interpretation

**Retina · Paediatric ophthalmology · Imaging**

**Status:** editorial draft awaiting medical review · **Language:** English (Arabic and German also available) · **Reading time:** approximately 12 minutes · **Literature checked:** 29 September 2026

> **Clinical takeaway:** The absence of a foveal pit alone does not predict poor vision. Assess the persistence of inner retinal layers, widening of the outer nuclear layer (ONL), and lengthening of cone outer segments on OCT. Then investigate gestational age and retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), pigmentation, nystagmus, anterior segment findings and, where appropriate, genetics. Structural grade informs prognosis but does not replace measured acuity or an aetiological diagnosis. [2, 3]

## Development of the fovea

The inner retinal layers move centrifugally; the foveal pit becomes visible in histological samples at about fetal week 25. Central cones pack more densely, and their inner and outer segments and axons lengthen substantially after birth. Pit shape matures earlier than cone specialisation: in a small postmortem histological series the pit appeared mature by around 15 months, while cone development continued through the early years of childhood. These are developmental observations rather than individual cut-offs. [1]

Handheld OCT allows developmental changes to be followed in preterm infants. An unusual foveal profile after preterm birth can reflect interrupted maturation. A single OCT cross-section therefore establishes neither a monogenic cause nor an individual visual prognosis. [1, 4, 5]

## A systematic OCT reading: Leicester grading

In every typical grade, inner retinal layers persist through the foveal centre. Three further landmarks define the grade: pit formation, ONL widening and cone outer-segment lengthening. [2, 3]

| Grade | Foveal pit | ONL widening | Outer-segment lengthening | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | shallow, present | present | present | cone specialisation visible |
| 2 | absent | present | present | cone specialisation despite absent pit |
| 3 | absent | present | absent | outer-segment specialisation not seen |
| 4 | absent | absent | absent | most severe typical developmental phenotype |

An **atypical** pattern includes disruption of the ellipsoid zone or photoreceptors, as may occur in achromatopsia; it is not simply a fifth step along the same developmental scale. The original case series included 69 affected people. Median visual acuities for grades 1–4 were 0.20, 0.44, 0.60 and 0.78 logMAR, respectively. These are group estimates in a mixed sample, not individual predictions. A larger multicentre cohort also associated morphology and genotype with vision, although some adjacent grades were not significantly distinct. [2, 3]

## One morphology, several aetiologies

**Prematurity.** In the Gutenberg Prematurity Eye Study, foveal hypoplasia was found in 2% of controls, 9% of those born at 33–36 gestational weeks, 18% at 29–32 weeks and 48% at ≤28 weeks. The proportions were 50% in the untreated ROP group and 82% of examined eyes in the treated ROP group. These selected strata do not give the prevalence among all preterm infants, and observational data do not show that ROP treatment caused hypoplasia. [4] A separate paediatric comparison included 57 preterm and 56 full-term children with hypoplasia. The preterm group more often had lower grades and mean acuity of 0.19 ± 0.27 logMAR versus 0.35 ± 0.36 logMAR in the full-term group. Selection and different underlying causes rule out an inference that prematurity protects vision. [5]

**Keep macular oedema of prematurity separate.** In another cohort, 32 of 94 preterm infants (34%) had cystoid macular oedema on neonatal OCT. Follow-up found no association with acuity or refractive error at 9 months, 2 years or 5 years. This percentage concerns **oedema**, not the prevalence or prognosis of foveal hypoplasia; a null association in one cohort does not establish universal harmlessness. [6]

**Albinism.** Subtle pigmentation changes do not exclude it. Relevant findings include iris transillumination, fundus hypopigmentation, nystagmus, foveal hypoplasia and abnormal visual pathway crossing on visual evoked potentials (VEP). In a retrospective cohort of 522 people, Kruijt and colleagues proposed major and minor clinical criteria: foveal hypoplasia grade ≥2 is a major criterion and grade 1 a minor criterion. These criteria require clinical synthesis; OCT alone is not diagnostic. Some established cases lack an individual classic sign. Ask about systemic features such as bleeding tendency when considering syndromic disease, including Hermansky–Pudlak syndrome. [3, 7, 8]

**Other genetic settings.** PAX6-related disorders, SLC38A8-related FHONDA, GPR143-related ocular albinism, FRMD7-related nystagmus and achromatopsia can produce overlapping central OCT findings with different accompanying phenotypes. FHONDA combines foveal hypoplasia and optic nerve misrouting without albinism and may include anterior segment dysgenesis. The multicentre genetic study analysed 907 people: 523 from 12 centres and 384 extracted from published datasets. Selection for molecular diagnosis and usable OCT prevents its genotype proportions from being interpreted as population prevalence. [3, 8]

## A practical diagnostic sequence

In a child with an abnormal foveal reflex, nystagmus or unexplained reduced vision, first assess age-appropriate best-corrected acuity, refraction and fundus. A well-centred macular OCT permits grading and inspection of the ellipsoid zone; handheld imaging can help in infants. Ask about gestational week, birth weight, ROP and its treatment. Examine iris transillumination, fundus pigmentation and anterior segment. Where clinically justified, consider VEP for misrouting and a clinical genetics referral. OCT grade helps frame prognosis, while the full phenotype guides investigation. [2–5, 7, 8]

## What the evidence supports and what remains uncertain

Foveal developmental anatomy and the OCT landmarks are well-described observations. Higher grade and worse acuity are associated **at group level**; nystagmus, amblyopia, refractive error, ROP and genotype also affect function. The foundational histology and grading samples were small; the genetic cohort was selectively assembled; prematurity cohorts may retain confounding. This evidence supports structured diagnosis and cautious counselling, not treatment of OCT morphology or a certain prediction for an individual child. [1–7]
